# Ethics, Consent, and Capacity — Applied Bioethics at the Bedside

**This guide owns** the *applied* clinical bioethics a clinician uses at the bedside: the **four
principles**, the elements and process of **informed consent**, **decision-making capacity** (the
Appelbaum four abilities) and how it differs from legal **competence**, **surrogate** decision-making
and **advance directives**, **confidentiality** and its limits, distributive **justice**, and the
**cultural variation** that the dominant framework under-weights. **It builds on**
`09-PREVENTION-AND-SCREENING` (shared decisions rest on consent and capacity) and `06-MULTIMORBIDITY-AND-GERIATRICS`
("what matters most" is the values input). **It explicitly defers** *normative ethical theory*
(deontology, consequentialism, virtue ethics) to `ethics/`; the *law itself* to `law/`; and the
*diseases/treatments* to `disease/`, `medicine/`, `pharmacology/`. This is a guide to *how clinicians
reason about consent, capacity, and ethical conflict* — it is **educational process only**: it is
**not** legal advice, **not** a tool for any reader to assess their own or anyone's capacity, and
**not** a substitute for a clinician, ethicist, or lawyer.

> **This module is an educational reference about *how clinical medicine reasons and
> how care is organized* — the cognitive and system architecture of the discipline.
> It is *not* medical advice. It does not diagnose, does not give treatment, dosing,
> or procedure instructions, does not give emergency or first-aid instructions, and
> is *not a substitute* for evaluation by a licensed clinician. Worked cases are
> illustrative teaching vignettes showing *how a clinician thinks*, not what any

## The Big Picture: Ethics Is Constraint Resolution, Not a Rulebook

The novice model is "follow the ethical rule." The expert model recognizes that the bedside is a
**multi-constraint problem with no single objective**: four principles that routinely conflict,
resolved case-by-case by weighing and specification, with **consent** and **capacity** as the
gating machinery that determines *who* gets to decide.

```
APPLIED BIOETHICS AS CONSTRAINT RESOLUTION  (this guide owns the reasoning, not the law)
==========================================================================
  FOUR PRINCIPLES (Section 1)  -- often in tension, no master rule
     autonomy | beneficence | non-maleficence | justice
        |
        v
  WHO DECIDES?  (the gating machinery)
     [ INFORMED CONSENT ]  disclosure + understanding + voluntariness + capacity + authorization (Sec 2)
              |
              v
     [ CAPACITY ]  the four abilities (Appelbaum, Section 3) -- decision-specific, clinical
              |
        has capacity? --YES--> the PATIENT decides (autonomy)
              |
              NO
              v
     [ SURROGATE + ADVANCE DIRECTIVES ]  substituted judgment, then best interests (Section 4)
challenges to all of the above:
     [ CONFIDENTIALITY + limits (Sec 5) ] [ JUSTICE / allocation (Sec 6) ] [ CULTURE (Sec 7) ]
==========================================================================
  There is no objective function that ranks the principles once and for all. The work is
  weighing them for THIS case, and the consent/capacity machinery decides whose weighing governs.
```

**Bridge (software).** The four principles are competing constraints in an optimization with no
global objective — you specify and weigh, you do not "solve." Consent is authorization with an
*informed* acknowledgment (a genuine ACK requires the grantor understood what they granted, not a
click-through). Capacity is a validation predicate on the decision-maker, scoped per-decision
(not a global role). Competence is a court's formal ruling (a different authority). Surrogate
decision-making is delegated execution under a mandate; confidentiality is access control with
defined exceptions; justice is fair scheduling of scarce resources.

## 1. The Four Principles

The dominant framework in Western clinical ethics is **principlism** (Beauchamp & Childress,
*Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, first edition 1979, now in its later editions): four mid-level
principles that clinicians balance rather than a single supreme rule.

| Principle | Core idea | Bedside expression |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | respect the person's right to self-determination | informed consent; the right to refuse |
| **Beneficence** | act in the patient's benefit | recommend what helps; promote wellbeing |
| **Non-maleficence** | avoid causing harm (*primum non nocere*) | weigh harms; avoid net-harmful interventions |
| **Justice** | fair distribution of benefits, risks, costs | equitable access; allocation of scarce resources |

```
  THE PRINCIPLES CONFLICT -- that is the point
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
   autonomy   vs   beneficence:  a capable patient refuses a beneficial treatment
   beneficence vs non-maleficence: an intervention that helps also harms (every treatment)
   autonomy   vs   justice:      one patient's demand vs fair use of shared resources
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  There is no fixed ranking. Resolution is SPECIFICATION (making a principle concrete for the
  case) + WEIGHING (balancing them here). Principlism is a shared vocabulary for the argument,
  not an algorithm that outputs the answer.
```

The framework's value is a **shared vocabulary** for naming the tension precisely; its limit is
that it does not rank the principles for you, and — as Section 7 notes — its heavy weighting of
*autonomy* reflects a Western-liberal stance that is not universal. `ethics/` owns the deeper
normative theories (deontology, consequentialism, virtue) that justify or critique principlism;
this guide owns its *application*.

## 2. Informed Consent — A Process, Not a Signature

**Informed consent** is the mechanism by which autonomy is operationalized. It is a *process*, not
a form, and it has distinct **elements** that all must hold for consent to be valid:

```
  ELEMENTS OF VALID INFORMED CONSENT  (all required; a signature alone is none of them)
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  (1) DISCLOSURE       nature of the intervention; risks, benefits, ALTERNATIVES,
                       and the option of no treatment
  (2) UNDERSTANDING    the person actually comprehends the disclosure
  (3) VOLUNTARINESS    the choice is free of coercion or undue influence
  (4) CAPACITY         the person can make THIS decision (Section 3)
  (5) AUTHORIZATION    the person actually decides / authorizes
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  A signed form with no understanding is not consent; it is a click-through. Consent is the
  informed authorization, of which the form is only evidence.
```

**Standards of disclosure** vary by jurisdiction and remain contested: some jurisdictions apply a
*reasonable-person* standard (disclose what a reasonable patient would want to know), others retain
the older *professional* standard (disclose what a reasonable clinician would disclose), and some
have moved toward a more *patient-specific* standard (what *this* particular patient would want to
know). Several jurisdictions have trended toward patient-centered standards, but no single standard
is universal — a legal detail owned by `law/`, noted here because it shapes what "disclosure" means. **Recognized exceptions** to obtaining
consent before acting include a genuine emergency in which a person cannot consent and delay would be
harmful (the law generally presumes consent to necessary care), an explicit **waiver** by the
patient, and — controversially and narrowly — "therapeutic privilege." These are described as *how
the framework handles edge cases*, not as permissions for any reader.

**Bridge (software).** Consent is authorization with an informed acknowledgment. A click-through "I
agree" that the user never read is the anti-pattern: authorization is only meaningful if the grantor
*understood the scope* of what they authorized. Real informed consent is the difference between a
genuine, scoped grant and a rubber-stamped EULA.

## 3. Decision-Making Capacity — The Appelbaum Four Abilities

**Capacity** is the clinical determination that a person can make a *particular* medical decision. The
operational standard (Appelbaum & Grisso, 1988; Appelbaum, *NEJM* 2007) is **four abilities**, all
assessed relative to the specific decision:

```
  THE FOUR ABILITIES  (Appelbaum) -- assessed for THIS decision, at THIS time
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  1 COMMUNICATE a choice        can express and hold a stable decision
  2 UNDERSTAND the information   grasps the relevant facts (condition, options, risks/benefits)
  3 APPRECIATE the situation     applies the facts to ONESELF ("this is my situation, and...")
  4 REASON about options         can weigh options and manipulate the information logically
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  APPRECIATION is frequently the ability selectively lost — and often the one hardest to detect:
  a person may UNDERSTAND facts in the
  abstract yet not APPRECIATE that they apply to them (e.g., due to denial or a delusion). The
  four are assessed together; failure of one relevant ability can indicate lacking capacity.
```

Four properties make capacity subtle and are the load-bearing content of this section:

- **Decision-specific, not global.** Capacity is for *this* decision at *this* time — a person may
  have capacity for a simple choice and lack it for a complex one. It is a per-operation
  authorization, not a global role.
- **Capacity (clinical) ≠ competence (legal).** *Capacity* is a clinician's functional
  determination; *competence* is a legal status decided by a court. A clinician assesses capacity; a
  judge adjudicates competence. Conflating them is a common error (and the legal side belongs to
  `law/`).
- **Risk-sensitive (a common, debated feature).** Many frameworks apply a *sliding scale*: the
  higher the stakes/risk of the decision, the more scrutiny the capacity assessment receives. This
  is standard practice but philosophically contested, and is presented here as *how clinicians
  operate*, not as settled truth.
- **Can fluctuate.** Capacity varies with reversible states (illness, medication effects, time of
  day), so it is re-assessed, and reversible causes are addressed before concluding a person lacks
  it.

**A capable refusal is still a decision.** The most important corollary: a person **with** capacity
may make a choice a clinician disagrees with — including refusing beneficial treatment — and autonomy
generally governs. Disagreement with the *choice* is not evidence of lacking capacity; capacity is
about the *process* of deciding, not the *content* of the decision. This guide describes how
clinicians assess these abilities; it is **not** a script for a reader to judge anyone's capacity.

**Bridge (systems).** The four abilities are a validation predicate on the decision-maker: can they
*receive* the inputs (understand), *bind them to their own state* (appreciate), *compute over them*
(reason), and *emit a stable output* (communicate a choice)? And it is scoped per-request — authorized
for this operation, re-checked because state can change — not a durable global permission.

## 4. Surrogates and Advance Directives

When a person lacks capacity for a decision, authority to decide moves — but the *goal* remains to
honor the person's own values as far as possible. Two instruments carry a person's autonomy forward:

| Instrument | What it is | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Living will** | a written statement of treatment wishes for future incapacity | expresses substantive preferences |
| **Durable power of attorney / healthcare proxy** | names a person to decide if capacity is lost | designates the decision-maker |

When capacity is absent, decisions follow an **ordered standard**:

```
  SURROGATE DECISION STANDARDS  (in priority order)
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  1 EXPRESSED WISHES     a valid advance directive that covers the situation -> follow it
  2 SUBSTITUTED JUDGMENT  "what would THIS person have chosen?" (from known values) -> decide as they would
  3 BEST INTERESTS        wishes unknown -> choose what a reasonable person would judge best for them
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  The standards are a fallback chain: use the most person-specific mandate available. A surrogate
  executes the PATIENT'S values (substituted judgment), not the surrogate's own preferences -- a
  distinction that is the crux of most surrogate conflicts.
```

Jurisdictions define a **hierarchy of surrogates** (who decides absent a named proxy — commonly a
spouse, then adult children, and so on); the exact order is legal content (`law/`). The key ethical
reasoning this guide owns is the **fallback chain**: a surrogate's job is *substituted judgment*
(decide as the patient would have), and only when the patient's wishes are genuinely unknown does the
standard drop to *best interests*. A surrogate substituting their *own* preferences for the patient's
is the characteristic failure — the delegated-execution analogue of an agent acting outside its
mandate.

**Bridge (systems).** An advance directive is a stored policy that executes when the principal is
unavailable; a healthcare proxy is a designated delegate. Substituted judgment is executing the
principal's mandate faithfully; best interests is the safe-default policy when the mandate is missing.

## 5. Confidentiality and Its Limits

**Confidentiality** — the duty to protect information a patient shares — is a pillar of the
therapeutic relationship and an expression of respect for autonomy and non-maleficence. But it is
**not absolute**; it is access control with *defined, narrow exceptions*.

```
  CONFIDENTIALITY: A STRONG DEFAULT WITH NARROW EXCEPTIONS
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  DEFAULT:   protect patient information; share only with the patient's authorization or
             with those involved in their care (minimum necessary)
  EXCEPTIONS (jurisdiction-specific, narrow):
     - serious, imminent risk of harm to an identifiable third party (duty to warn/protect)
     - mandatory reporting (certain infectious diseases, some categories of harm)
     - public-health / legal obligations defined by law
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  The exceptions are LIMITED and rule-bound, not clinician discretion at large. When they apply,
  disclosure is the MINIMUM necessary for the purpose.
```

The canonical (dated, jurisdiction-specific) example of the harm-to-others limit is *Tarasoff v.
Regents of the University of California* (California, 1976), which established a "duty to protect" an
identifiable person facing a serious threat — a legal precedent owned by `law/`, cited here to show
that confidentiality yields to a *narrow, rule-bound* exception rather than to open discretion. The
ethical reasoning is a constrained balance: a strong default duty to the patient, overridden only when
a defined, weighty countervailing duty applies, and then only to the minimum extent necessary.

**Bridge (systems).** Confidentiality is an access-control policy: deny by default, grant on a
need-to-know (care-team) basis, and log narrow, rule-defined exceptions with a minimum-disclosure
principle. "Break-glass" access exists but is constrained and auditable, not a general override.

## 6. Justice — Allocation and Equity at the Bedside

The fourth principle, **justice**, is where the individual encounter meets the collective. At the
bedside it appears as **distributive justice**: the fair allocation of finite resources (time,
specialist access, scarce treatments) and equity across patients. It creates a structural tension the
other three principles do not: a clinician is both the individual patient's advocate *and* a steward
of shared resources.

```
  JUSTICE AT THE BEDSIDE  (individual advocacy vs collective stewardship)
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
   patient-level duty:   do the best for THIS patient (beneficence + autonomy)
        vs
   system-level duty:    fair use of shared, finite resources across ALL patients
  ----------------------------------------------------------------
  Allocation FRAMEWORKS (equal opportunity, greatest benefit, priority to worst-off, etc.) are
  normative theory owned by ethics/; POPULATION allocation/financing is public-health/. This guide
  owns only the recognition that the tension is real and structural at the individual encounter.
```

The deep theories of what "fair" means (egalitarian, utilitarian, prioritarian, and so on) are
`ethics/`'s territory; *population-level* allocation, rationing policy, and financing are
`public-health/`'s. This guide owns the bedside recognition that justice is a genuine, structural
constraint on individual care — not a reason to shortchange the patient in front of one, but a
reminder that "everything possible for this patient" is not a value-free default when resources are
shared.

## 7. Cultural Variation — The Framework Is Not Universal

The entire apparatus above is **Western-liberal and autonomy-forward**, and a peer reader must hold
that explicitly (the caveat flagged in guide 00). Principlism's heavy weighting of individual
autonomy, the emphasis on direct disclosure to the patient, and the individual-consent model are
*cultural choices*, not universal facts.

| Dimension | The dominant (Western) frame | Common variation |
|---|---|---|
| Locus of decision | the individual patient | family- or community-mediated decisions |
| Disclosure norm | full, direct disclosure to the patient | in some cultures, information mediated through family; non-disclosure preferences |
| Weighting of principles | autonomy often first among equals | beneficence/non-maleficence or community weighted more heavily |
| Definition of "best" | individual self-determination | relational, familial, or communal wellbeing |

The reasoning move this guide teaches is **not** to abandon the framework, and **not** to universalize
it, but to *recognize its provenance* and elicit how a given patient and family locate decision-making
authority and disclosure — itself an expression of respect (a culturally humble application of
autonomy). Normative debate about which frame is *right* belongs to `ethics/`; this guide owns the
clinical humility that the four principles are one tradition among several.

## Fully Worked Case — Consent, Capacity, and a Surrogate Path (illustrative, fictional)

All details are invented to show the *reasoning*; nothing here is legal advice or a capacity-assessment
procedure for any reader. Specifics are abstract (`disease/`, `medicine/`, `law/`).

**Setup.** A fictional patient, **P**, faces a values-sensitive decision. There is genuine uncertainty
about whether P can make *this* decision right now.

**Step 1 — consent as a process (Section 2).** A clinician works through the elements: disclosure
(nature, risks, benefits, **alternatives**, and the no-treatment option), checks understanding and
voluntariness, and treats a signature as evidence of — not a substitute for — informed authorization.

**Step 2 — assess capacity for THIS decision (Section 3).** The clinician evaluates the four abilities:
whether P can **communicate** a stable choice, **understand** the relevant facts, **appreciate** that
they apply to P's own situation, and **reason** across the options. Because the stakes are high, the
assessment is more careful (risk-sensitivity), and reversible contributors (illness, medication
effects) are addressed first, since capacity can fluctuate. Crucially, if P **has** capacity, a
refusal the clinician disagrees with is still P's decision — capacity is about process, not the
content of the choice.

**Step 3 — if capacity is lacking, follow the fallback chain (Section 4).** If P is found to lack
capacity for this decision, authority moves along the ordered standard: a valid advance directive
covering the situation is followed; absent that, a surrogate exercises **substituted judgment** (what
*P* would have chosen from P's known values, guide 06's "what matters most"); only if P's wishes are
genuinely unknown does the standard drop to **best interests**. The surrogate is reasoning as *P*,
not imposing their own preference.

**Step 4 — a confidentiality wrinkle (Section 5).** Information relevant to the decision is protected
by default and shared only with the care team and authorized surrogate on a minimum-necessary basis;
a disclosure would require a narrow, rule-bound exception, not clinician discretion.

**Step 5 — cultural humility (Section 7).** The clinician elicits how P and P's family locate
decision-making authority and disclosure, rather than assuming the individual-autonomy default — a
culturally humble application of the same respect that grounds consent.

**What the case shows.** Consent treated as informed authorization; capacity assessed as the four
abilities for *this* decision and distinguished from legal competence; a fallback chain (expressed
wishes → substituted judgment → best interests) when capacity is absent; confidentiality as a
default-deny policy with narrow exceptions; and explicit cultural humility — the applied-bioethics
reasoning this guide owns, described as clinician process and not as a tool for any reader.

## Reader Tasks (answerable from this guide)

1. **Name the conflict, not the answer.** Given a scenario where a capable patient refuses beneficial
   care, identify which principles conflict (autonomy vs beneficence) and explain why principlism
   supplies a vocabulary, not a ranking. (Section 1.)
2. **Audit an informed-consent process.** Given a signed form with no evidence of understanding,
   explain which of the five consent elements are missing and why the signature alone is not consent.
   (Section 2.)
3. **Apply the four abilities.** Given a fictional decision, describe how a clinician would assess
   communicate/understand/appreciate/reason, why appreciation is frequently the ability selectively
   lost (and often the hardest to detect), and why capacity is decision-specific, not global. (Section 3.)
4. **Distinguish capacity from competence, and walk the surrogate chain.** Explain the clinical/legal
   distinction, and order the surrogate standards (expressed wishes → substituted judgment → best
   interests) with the failure mode each prevents. (Sections 3–4.)
5. **Bound a confidentiality exception.** Given a possible harm-to-others situation, explain why
   confidentiality is a strong default with narrow, rule-bound exceptions and what "minimum necessary"
   means — noting this is not legal advice. (Section 5.)

## Common Confusion Points

**"Capacity and competence are the same thing."** No — *capacity* is a clinician's functional,
decision-specific determination (can this person make *this* decision now?); *competence* is a legal
status decided by a court. A clinician assesses capacity; a judge adjudicates competence. The legal
side belongs to `law/`.

**"A patient who refuses beneficial treatment lacks capacity."** No — capacity is about the *process*
of deciding (understand, appreciate, reason, communicate), not the *content* of the choice. A capable
person may make a choice the clinician disagrees with, and autonomy generally governs. Disagreement is
not evidence of incapacity.

**"Consent is the signed form."** The form is *evidence*; consent is the *informed authorization* —
disclosure, understanding, voluntariness, capacity, and an actual decision. A signature with no
understanding is a click-through, not consent.

**"A surrogate decides what they think is best."** A surrogate is meant to exercise **substituted
judgment** — deciding as the *patient* would have from the patient's known values — and only falls back
to **best interests** when those wishes are genuinely unknown. Substituting the surrogate's own
preferences is the characteristic failure.

**"Confidentiality is absolute."** It is a strong default with *narrow, rule-bound* exceptions (serious
imminent harm to an identifiable person, mandatory reporting, defined legal duties), and even then only
the minimum necessary is disclosed. It is access control with audited exceptions, not clinician
discretion at large.

**"These principles are universal.”** They are one tradition — Western-liberal and autonomy-forward.
Where decision authority sits (individual vs family/community) and disclosure norms vary by culture.
The reasoning move is to recognize the framework's provenance and elicit the patient's own frame, not
to universalize it — and this guide is educational process only, not legal or personal advice.
